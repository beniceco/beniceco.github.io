Will this USB-C charger run my laptop?

A laptop that charges over USB-C will accept any USB-C charger, but "accept" and "keep up" are different things. Compare the wattage printed on the laptop's own adapter with the charger or power bank you want to use.

What the numbers mean

Laptops negotiate power over USB Power Delivery: the charger announces what it can supply and the laptop takes what it needs up to that limit. A 45 W charger on a 65 W laptop is safe. The laptop simply charges more slowly, and under heavy load it may still drain, just more slowly than on battery alone.

Below roughly 30 W many laptops show "slow charger" and some refuse to charge while running. Gaming laptops with adapters above 140 W usually charge over USB-C only as a backup, if at all.

The cable is often the real limit

A standard USB-C cable is rated for 3 A, which is 60 W at 20 V. Above that, the cable needs an electronic marker chip that tells the charger it can carry 5 A. A 100 W charger with an unmarked cable delivers 60 W, so check the cable before blaming the charger.

Smaller chargers got better

Chargers built with gallium nitride transistors switch faster and waste less heat, which is why a 65 W charger can now be the size of an old phone brick. That makes it realistic to carry one charger for phone and laptop together, as long as its total output covers both.

Power banks

A power bank rated for laptop use needs a USB-C PD output of at least 45 W, and enough capacity to matter: a typical 50 to 60 Wh laptop battery is roughly a 20,000 mAh bank's worth after conversion losses. Airlines also limit spare batteries by watt-hours, which the FAA's PackSafe guidance sets out.
